# Cut waiting, show honest status, guard the plant

Users judge this app on two things: does it do what it says, and does it answer at once. The research agrees on what "answers at once" means. A tap needs a visible response within about **0.1 s**. A wait under **1 s** needs no loading sign at all. Waits of 1 to 10 s need a placeholder. Longer waits need real progress. Your current rules already follow the best-supported advice: show the layout at once, show cached data at once, show a skeleton only after 300 ms, and show "—" rather than a guess. The real gaps are elsewhere. Your own launch animation adds about **1.45 s** to every cold start, can't be skipped, and blocks taps while it runs. That is above Google's 1,000 ms guidance. Skeletons have no minimum display time, so a load that finishes at about 320 ms flashes a placeholder for one frame. The planned first run has three likely drop-off points: losing the guest's plant at the account sheet, the email-confirmation detour, and asking for permissions in the wrong place. "Works properly" weighs more than "feels fast". In one-star and two-star reviews, broken features and crashes are the top complaints. For a device that waters by itself, a reading that looks live but isn't, or watering that doesn't happen with no reason given, counts as "broken". A QA pipeline can check most of these rules automatically. It cannot measure satisfaction itself. A green pipeline means "nothing we can see is broken or slow". It does not predict a score. Several popular numbers in this field are folklore, and this report marks them.

## Speed has three bands, and the launch animation sits outside them

The 0.1 s / 1 s / 10 s limits are old and come from desktop computers. They come from expert summaries (Miller 1968, Card 1991, Nielsen 1993), not from modern phone experiments. **0.1 s feels instant. Up to 1 s keeps the user's flow, though they notice the delay. At 10 s attention is gone, and progress with an end time is needed** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)). They have lasted because every current platform standard lands on the same bands. Google's RAIL model says respond to input within 100 ms. It also says users lose focus after about 1,000 ms ([web.dev RAIL](https://web.dev/articles/rail)). The web metric INP counts a tap response as good at **≤ 200 ms** and poor above 500 ms ([web.dev INP](https://web.dev/articles/inp)). On Android, a frame has **16 ms** to draw at 60 Hz. A frame that takes over **700 ms** counts as "frozen", and after 5 s with no response the app is "not responding" (ANR) ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals/render)). The popular "Doherty threshold" of 400 ms is not reliable. No one could find that figure in the 1982 IBM source, and that work measured office-terminal productivity, not how happy phone users are ([Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/doherty-threshold/)). The 400 ms worth using is Apple's: **draw the first frame within 400 ms of launch** ([WWDC19 session 423](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/423/)).

App start has its own limits. Android vitals flags a cold start of **≥ 5 s**, a warm start of ≥ 2 s and a hot start of ≥ 1.5 s as excessive. These are measured to the first frame. Google counts the time until the app is actually usable as just as important ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals/launch-time)). Google also says its own user research shows an animated splash makes start-up *feel* shorter. But it recommends the animation **not exceed 1,000 ms on phones**, and says it must be skippable when the app is ready sooner ([Android splash screens](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/launch/splash-screen)). That research is unpublished. The claim fits an animation that *covers* real loading time. It doesn't fit one that *adds* time.

Here is what the code does. `LaunchAnimation.tsx` draws for about 700 ms, rests for `REST_MS = 500`, then fades out for 250 ms. `useLaunchGate` starts the fade only after the animation has finished *and* the session has loaded. So the overlay stays up for at least about 1.45 s, even when the app is ready earlier. While it is up it blocks taps (`pointerEvents="auto"` until the fade starts). "Once per app run" means every cold start, and phones often close apps in the background. So returning users keep paying this cost. NN/g's advice on motion points the same way: most UI animation should last 100–500 ms, and the more often people see it, the shorter it should be ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration)). Reduce Motion is handled well: about 300 ms plus a 150 ms fade.

## Skeletons help less than having the layout ready

The controlled evidence on skeleton screens is thin, and the studies disagree. A 2018 conference study found a skeleton version was rated faster and easier than a spinner version ([NN/g summary of Mejtoft et al.](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/)). An online test with **136 people** found the opposite. There the skeleton *felt slowest*: 2.82 s against 2.41 s for a spinner and 2.29 s for a blank screen ([Viget](https://viget.com/articles/a-bone-to-pick-with-skeleton-screens)). Neither study is strong. What the expert guidance agrees on is timing. Under about 1 s, no placeholder at all, because "a quick flash of a placeholder can make users feel they can't keep up". From 2 to 10 s, use a skeleton for full pages. Over 10 s, show a progress bar with an estimate. Shimmer effects can distract ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/)). The likely real benefit of a skeleton is that the layout is already in place and nothing jumps when data arrives. That makes your "full layout immediately" rule worth more than the skeleton's look.

Your 300 ms delay is a sensible convention, not a tested number. No study compares 200, 300 and 500 ms. Design systems that delay loaders by 300 ms usually add a second rule: **once a loader shows, keep it for at least about 400 ms**. Otherwise a fast load causes "visual disruption resulting in worse experience than showing nothing at all" ([Learning Equality design system](https://design-system.learningequality.org/loaders)). `useDelayedVisible` in `Skeleton.tsx` has the delay but no minimum, so a 320 ms load flashes a skeleton for about 20 ms. The 150 ms fade-in is within NN/g's 100–500 ms range for UI motion and changes only opacity, which is cheap to animate. One small point: `FadeIn` uses `useNativeDriver: false`. Your project rule says to use the native driver where possible, and opacity can run on it. Check whether this was a deliberate choice for the web build.

There is no controlled study on showing cached data instantly or on "optimistic" updates (showing a change before the server confirms it). The material is all engineering opinion. Practitioners agree on one thing: avoid optimistic updates for actions that matter or can't be undone, because a visible rollback "is jarring and potentially damaging to trust" ([skill-wiki](https://skill-wiki.github.io/atom/frontend-design/fact-optimistic-update-tradeoff)). For this app, showing cached data instantly is still the biggest single speed win. Its specific risk is old device data that looks live. The code already deals with this. `deviceStatus` in `src/devices/status.ts` switches to "Not heard from since …" once the last check-in is more than three sync intervals old. So a cached "Doing well" can't outlive its data. Keep that, and test it.

## Broken features cost more than slow ones, and a silent device counts as broken

The firmest evidence on reliability comes from Google Play policy. If more than **1.09%** of daily users see a crash, or more than **0.47%** see an "app not responding" error (over 28 days), Play may show the app less and put a warning on its store page ([Android vitals](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals)). Studies of real reviews point the same way. Researchers hand-sorted **6,390** one-star and two-star iOS reviews. Broken features, feature requests and crashes were the most common complaints. About **11% blamed a recent update** ([Khalid et al., IEEE Software](https://swag.uwaterloo.ca/publications/what-do-mobile-app-users-complain-about.html)). A second study of apps on both iOS and Android also found broken features the top complaint on both platforms ([Hu et al., EMSE 2018](https://sail.cs.queensu.ca/data/pdfs/EMSE2018_StudyingTheConsistencyOfStarRatingsAndTheComplaintsIn1&2StarUserReviewsForTopFreeCrossPlatformAndroidAndIOSApps.pdf)). One vendor claims you need about 99.7% crash-free sessions to hold 3 stars and 99.85% to pass 4.5 stars. Its method is not published, so treat these as weak numbers ([Luciq](https://www.luciq.ai/mobile-app-stability-outlook-2025)). For your app, "doesn't work" will mostly mean no crash at all. It will mean a "Resume watering" that did nothing, or a reading that was stale, or a plan that never ran.

Good error handling is well agreed on, but the evidence is expert guidance, not measured effects. Show the error next to its cause. Use plain words with no codes. Say exactly what went wrong and offer a fix. Never blame the user, and skip humour on errors that repeat. Keep what the user typed ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines/)). Shneiderman adds that a failed action should leave the screen as it was. Being able to undo "relieves anxiety" ([Shneiderman](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/ben/goldenrules.html)). Your "short message + Try again" pattern meets this only when trying again can actually fix the problem. For physical faults the message must name the check to make, and `status.ts` already does that ("Check the stake is in the soil and the tube isn't kinked").

For a device that acts on its own, the goal is *calibrated* trust: people should trust the device exactly as much as it deserves, no more and no less. Over-trust and under-trust both cause failures ([Lee & See 2004, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15151155/)). Secondary summaries of that paper name three things that build trust. These are performance (what it did), process (how it works) and purpose (why it exists). Its full text was not checked here. Explaining *why* automation might fail raised trust and use in three experiments ([Dzindolet et al., via review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720814547570)). Aviation research calls the worst failure an "automation surprise". The user can no longer answer "What's it doing? Why? What will it do next?" ([Woods](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7f1f6aed915d74e6228865/Prof_David_Woods__Ohio_State_University.pdf)). In a 2026 diary study of smart-home alerts, late or false alerts hurt trust. Warnings that stayed on after the fix drove users to turn off whole categories, and they rarely turned them back on. Users wanted to hear when things were back to normal ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/smart-home-notifications/)). Your "—" rule and the "Not heard from" status are exactly what calibration asks for. Two things are still missing. One is a visible "why it isn't watering now", together with the last dose. The other is a clear follow-up after "Resume watering". Shneiderman's rule says rare, major actions need a bigger response ([Shneiderman](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/ben/goldenrules.html)). Here that means an immediate "Sent", then "Watering resumed" once the module picks the command up. Most of this research comes from aviation and security homes, where far more is at stake than a plant. How well it carries over to a self-watering pot is a reasoned guess.

## The first run should deliver value first, then ask quietly

Letting people sign up *after* they have seen the app working has one well-known data point. Duolingo moved sign-up "back a few steps" and saw "about a 20% increase in DAUs" (daily active users). Swapping a red "Discard my progress" button for a quiet "Later" did better. A hard sign-up wall only worked after earlier skippable ones had prepared users for it ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker)). This is a report from inside the company, with no sample sizes, and the oft-quoted "10% → 12%" figure can't be traced to a source. Your order is scan, result, name and place, then "Keep {name}". That matches the pattern. The biggest risk is the guest's plant getting lost. If it doesn't survive closing the app, signing up and confirming the email, the value-first idea backfires. Email confirmation sends the user to their mail app right after they've put in effort, which makes it the likeliest point to lose them. Measure it rather than assume it's fine. Baymard finds the total number of fields matters more than the number of steps ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/ecommerce-checkout-usability-report-and-benchmark)), so keep the account sheet to one or two fields.

The best evidence on permission timing is a controlled study with **473 people** and 30 mock apps. Asking in context always raised the share who said yes. Adding a reason raised it further ([Elbitar et al., USENIX Security 2021](https://group.cispa.io/bugiel/publication/elbitar-usenix-21/elbitar-usenix-21.pdf)). Android's own guidance says the same: ask when the user triggers the feature, explain the value first, and maybe wait until users know the app ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/notifications/notification-permission)). Your plan already asks for the camera after "Scan a plant" and for notifications after module setup, which is right. Users who skip the module should get no notification request in the first run. They have nothing to be notified about yet. Push vendors' opt-in figures ("2–3x higher with priming") are marketing with no data behind them.

Progress bars are weaker than people assume. A review of **32 randomised experiments** (on web surveys, not apps) found they don't reliably reduce drop-off. They raised it when early progress looked slow, and lowered it when early progress looked fast ([Villar et al. 2013](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/2163855)). With about ten screens in the first run, one long bar would start slow. Two short ones (add plant, set up module) avoid that. "Choice overload" averages out to almost nothing across 50 experiments ([Scheibehenne et al. 2010](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf)). It only matters when people are unsure what they want ([Chernev et al. 2015](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c5373-70ae2e6347848385bf9476552c3afd7fa54acd274975d20e58ce012ae97619d43)). Light level is the one choice users may not know, so give it a default and a one-line hint.

The "peak-end rule" says people remember an experience mostly by its best or worst moment and its ending. In software tests the evidence is mixed. The predicted effect held in only one of three form-filling conditions. The *ending* did measurably shape preference, and the beginning didn't ([Cockburn et al. 2017](https://ir.canterbury.ac.nz/bitstream/10092/14654/2/accepted-version.pdf)). So make "The tank is full" and the Plants home that follows calm and finished, not a skeleton. But a good ending won't rescue a broken middle. Attractive design correlates with *expected* ease of use (r ≈ 0.59). The causal evidence mostly runs the other way: unusable systems get rated less beautiful ([Tractinsky 1997](https://chi1997.acm.org/proceedings/paper/nt.html); [Tractinsky et al. 2000](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/pluginfile.php/1645430/mod_folder/content/0/Tractinsky%20%282000%29.pdf)). For a watering device, accurate readings and pump safety are must-haves. Polish can't make up for a failure there. No data was found that measures how much accessibility adds to satisfaction. Treat it as a must-have for the people who rely on it. Your existing rules (no cap on font scaling, static shimmer under Reduce Motion) are the baseline to test.

## A pipeline can prove "works and fast", not "satisfied"

Satisfaction questionnaires need real people. A script can measure what those scores tend to follow: whether the task got done, how long it took, and how many errors happened. The link is real but only moderate. The one-question ease rating (SEQ) predicts about **44% of the variation in completion and 28% of task time**. The broader SUS score captures only **3–6%** of observed behaviour ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/why-collect-task-and-study-metrics/)). About 14% of users who *fail* a task still rate it very easy ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/?p=502)). So present "projected satisfaction" as a set of pass/fail limits and budgets. A predicted score would be made up until you can calibrate it against real survey answers after launch. AI reviewers can't fill the gap. GPT-4o found only **21%** of the issues human experts found ([Guerino et al., preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.16345)). Multimodal models matched experts on looks but rated usability too generously, by 1–3 points ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-30549-7_12)). Use them as advisory comments only, never as a gate.

Timing checks only work if they control for noise. Reassure compares your branch with a baseline on the same machine, one after the other. Under 5% random change is normal, and 10% or more means the machine is too noisy to trust ([Reassure](https://oss.callstack.com/reassure/docs/methodology)). Lighthouse needs the median of five runs on dedicated cores ([Lighthouse](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/blob/main/docs/variability.md)). Timings from emulators on shared CI machines should be trends, not gates. Expo documents Maestro on EAS Workflows for end-to-end runs on Android emulators and iOS simulators ([Expo](https://docs.expo.dev/eas/workflows/examples/e2e-tests.md)). The research notes estimate iOS CI minutes cost about ten times Linux minutes, so run iOS nightly. After launch, both stores limit in-app rating prompts. Android forbids asking "do you like the app?" first, and iOS shows the prompt at most three times a year ([Android](https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/in-app-review); [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/storekit/skstorereviewcontroller/requestreview(in:).md)). Ask only after a success, such as the first automatic watering.

## Conclusion

Measured against the owner's definition, the app's biggest self-inflicted slowness is its own brand moment. It loses more speed to the launch animation than to anything else. Cutting the 500 ms rest and letting the overlay leave as soon as the app is ready is the cheapest speed win available. Its biggest "doesn't work" risk isn't a crash. It's the device going quiet, or a first-run plant disappearing at the account sheet. Both can be caught before release: the first with the simulator, the second with a scripted web run. The psychology research mostly confirms rules you already have. Its new contributions are an explicit "why not watering / what next" view, a confirmed hand-off for "Resume watering", and the habit of treating every cited percentage (opt-in lifts, crash-to-rating thresholds, the 400 ms Doherty figure) as folklore until proven.

The honest limit: no validated model turns pipeline numbers into a satisfaction score. The checks below define "nothing broken, nothing slow" precisely. Whether that equals "satisfied" for this product will only be known once the first in-app ease ratings come back and can be compared with telemetry.
